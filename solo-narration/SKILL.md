# Solo Narration

Turn a source or a topic into a Persian narration script for a 14–18 minute narrative documentary or video essay on the Solo («سولو») YouTube channel. The script is written to be **heard, not read** and should feel cinematic, immersive, intelligent, suspenseful, and story-driven. Its narrative approach must emerge from the subject itself. Do not assume that every topic needs a human protagonist, one climactic scene, a circular structure, or a two-sided question at the end. Some subjects are best told through a person; others through an event, process, mechanism, contradiction, discovery, decision, or consequence.

Instructions are in English; the script itself is always Persian. Persian phrases below are templates to reuse verbatim or adapt.

## Before writing: study the Narration-Style folder (mandatory)

The folder `D:\.YT-vault\Skills\solo-narration\Narration-Style` (inside this skill's folder) is the channel's style reference. **Before drafting any narration, read every document in it carefully and in full:**

- `Style-Guide.md`: the complete Persian guide to the house style, tone, structure, formatting and orthography, with annotated examples from both samples.
- `Sample-1.md`: «کاپیتان بایکوت؛ چرا امپراتوری بریتانیا برای برداشت شلغم ارتش فرستاد؟» (about 2,130 words).
- `Sample-2.md`: «سوررئالیسم؛ چگونه رؤیاهای یک شاعر پاریسی دشمن فاشیسم شد؟» (about 2,460 words).
- Any other file later added to the folder.

Read the two samples as a writer, not as a skimmer. Study their Persian prose, rhythm, suspense, transitions, paragraph length, use of contrast, and spoken clarity. Notice their openings, «اما» pivots, date slugs, callbacks, and endings, but distinguish reusable stylistic strengths from subject-specific structural choices. Your script should feel like a third sibling in voice and craft, not necessarily in plot structure. List the folder first rather than assuming its contents.

Both samples predate the no-CTA rule: they contain a subscribe line, a related-video plug (Sample-2) and a closing like/comment line, and Sample-1 names the channel «پروفایل». Never imitate those parts. When the samples and this file disagree, this file wins on the rules marked **always** or **never**: no CTAs, no Latin script, no parentheses, Persian digits. Everywhere else, follow the samples and the style guide.

If the folder cannot be reached, for example when the computer isn't linked, say so to the user and continue from the rules in this file.

## Output contract

- One Markdown file: line 1 is `# <title>`, then continuous prose paragraphs separated by blank lines. No subheadings, lists, bold, tables or Latin-script words inside the script.
- **Never write calls to action**: no subscribe request, no promotion of another video, no closing "comment, like and share" line. The script ends on the closing question. The editor adds CTAs later.
- Length: 2,000–2,500 words unless the user gives a target («حدود ۲۰۰۰ کلمه» means 1,900–2,150). Count words, excluding the title, before delivering.
- Filename = the title, minus Windows-forbidden characters (`: ? / \ * " < > |`). Use Persian `؛` instead of `:` and Persian `؟` instead of `?`.
- In the user's vault, save to `D:\.YT-vault\1-Content\4-Narration`.

## Topics: let the subject determine the story

The channel is not limited to assassinations, espionage, biographies, or historical incidents. It can cover history, politics, science, technology, medicine, art, literature, pseudoscience, conspiracy claims, culture, philosophy, social systems, and abstract ideas. A subject does not need a conventional protagonist to become a compelling narrative. Topic sources usually live in `D:\.YT-vault\Topic-Bank\` (for example `_Several`). Many are long Wikipedia-style dumps, and some are empty stubs that hold only a title.

| Category | Possible narrative entry points (choose what genuinely fits) |
|---|---|
| History and political events | A documented scene, decision, turning point, chain of events, institution, or affected group |
| Biographies and enigmatic figures | The person's choices, contradictions, relationships, discoveries, or consequences |
| Power, punishment and human rights | A documented case, institutional mechanism, policy, testimony, or pattern of consequences |
| Movements, art and literature | An artwork, manifesto, creative process, ideological contradiction, historical moment, or a relevant person |
| Science, cosmos and the mind | An experiment, observation, mechanism, discovery, unresolved question, or researcher |
| Pseudoscience, conspiracies and mysteries | A claim, the evidence offered for it, a test, a contradiction, or how the claim spread |
| Technology and society | How a system works, a consequential decision, a documented case, a limitation, or an unintended consequence |
| Myth, religion and culture | A ritual, text, historical transformation, contested interpretation, or a person when relevant |
| Survival stories | A verified survivor account, a sequence of events, environmental conditions, or a critical decision |

**Find the natural narrative entry point.** It may be a real person, event, process, mechanism, contradiction, paradox, discovery, decision, question, place, object, document, or consequence. These are options, not a checklist. Never choose a human anchor by default or add a famous politician, celebrity, victim, inventor, or witness merely to personalize a conceptual topic.

**Never invent a protagonist.** Do not fabricate a person, dialogue, thoughts, or scene and present them as historical fact. A clearly labeled hypothetical example may be used when it genuinely clarifies a mechanism, but do not confuse it with a documented event. If a group is the best narrative subject, it does not have to be represented by one named member. Use an individual member only when the evidence supports the detail and the person helps tell the story.

## Three governing principles

1. **Narrative, not report.** Organize information into a meaningful progression of events, ideas, mechanisms, decisions, discoveries, or consequences. A human character is one option, not a requirement.
2. **Suspense before information, when appropriate.** Create curiosity through genuine uncertainty, conflict, contradiction, stakes, or a question. Do not manufacture suspense where the material does not support it.
3. **Fair judgment.** Distinguish facts from interpretation. Present competing views when they genuinely exist and matter; do not force a false balance or hand every conclusion to a binary dilemma.

## Workflow

1. **Study the Narration-Style folder** (`Style-Guide.md`, `Sample-1.md`, `Sample-2.md`) in full, as described above. Do not skip this step, even for a short rewrite.
2. **Check the source.** First look in `D:\.YT-vault\1-Content\3-Research\` for a research dossier on the topic made by the solo-research skill. If one exists, use it as the primary source: section 8 (narration toolkit) gives the anchor, climactic scenes, quote and number banks, ironies and the two closing views, and section 4 gives the characters. Use only material it labels قطعی or پشتوانه قوی as fact, and attribute anything محل مناقشه. If no dossier exists and the source is empty or thin, suggest running solo-research first, or research the topic with web search and tell the user what you used.
3. **Extract the useful material.** Gather the relevant timeline, people or groups, mechanisms, decisions, striking numbers, direct quotes, contradictions, and consequences. Do not force irrelevant categories into the notes.
4. **Identify the central tension.** Ask what changes, develops, fails, spreads, or comes into conflict; what is genuinely at stake; and what question would make a listener want to understand what happens next. Do not begin by asking who the protagonist should be.
5. **Choose the narrative approach.** Select the strongest documented person, event, process, mechanism, contradiction, discovery, decision, or question. Do not invent a character or climactic scene.
6. **Choose or adapt a structure.** The eight-stage ring structure below is one option, not a mandatory template. Build the word budget around the actual material.
7. **Select and cut.** Keep material that advances the central question or deepens understanding. Cut repetition and irrelevant background, but do not remove context essential to accuracy.
8. **Draft the body before finalizing the opening.** This ensures the opening promises a story the body actually delivers. The opening and ending may be refined last.
9. **Rewrite for the ear.** Read sentences as if aloud, split anything that would leave the narrator breathless, and simplify words that trip.
10. **Final pass:** word count, fact check, and the checklist at the end of this file.

## Narrative structure: choose, adapt, or combine

There is no mandatory structure for every topic. Choose the form that best serves the material: chronological progression, biography, investigation, mechanism-and-consequence, cause-and-effect, converging narratives, conceptual progression, comparison, or a hybrid. The narrative should feel coherent, and each section should advance the question or deepen the listener's understanding.

### Optional eight-stage ring structure

Use this proven structure when beginning with a compelling scene and returning to it later adds meaning. It is an option, not a rule. Do not force a climax, a return to the opening, or an artificial circular ending when the subject is better served by another structure. The budgets below are illustrative for 2,000–2,500 words.

| # | Stage | Words | Purpose | Template |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening | 150–250 | Establish a compelling question, scene, contradiction, or uncertainty | Choose the form that fits the subject |
| 2 | Orientation | 40–100 | Clarify what the audience is encountering and why it matters, if needed | A name, mechanism, event, or key distinction |
| 3 | Hook | 50–80 | Sharpen the central question and stakes | A question, contradiction, or unresolved outcome |
| 4 | Narrative promise | 30–60 | Establish the journey without overexplaining the answer | A concise statement of what the script will investigate |
| 5 | Background | 350–500 | Provide the necessary historical, scientific, political, or conceptual context | Use only the context the story needs |
| 6 | Development | 700–900 | Follow turning points, mechanisms, conflicts, discoveries, or decisions | Build a meaningful progression |
| 7 | Culmination or return | 300–450 | Present the decisive development, consequence, or return to the opening when useful | Do not manufacture a climax |
| 8 | Consequences and conclusion | 300–400 | Explain the outcome, significance, and relevant interpretations | End with a conclusion or question suited to the evidence |

The development may move through wider context, the rise of an opposing force, a sequence of decisions, a mechanism's operation, or the consequences of a discovery. Use only the movements the subject actually contains.

The samples often end with character outcomes, legacy, attributed viewpoints, historical irony, and a final question. Borrow these devices when they fit, but do not force a death, legacy paragraph, two-sided judgment, ironic line, or dilemma onto every topic.

**Transitions without headings** (scripts have no subheadings):
- Open a paragraph with «اما» when the direction genuinely changes. The samples' frequency is a reference, not a quota.
- End a paragraph on a meaningful implication, discovery, or unresolved point when appropriate; not every paragraph needs a cliffhanger.
- Foreshadow only when the later narrative delivers on the anticipation. Avoid generic promises such as «اما طوفانی بزرگ در راه بود» unless the material justifies them.

## Opening and narrative hook

The first 200 words deserve particular care. Their purpose is to create interest and establish a reason to continue listening. The opening does not have to depict the climax, contain a person, begin with a location slug, or include a fixed number of sensory details. Include these elements only when they genuinely strengthen the story.

### First, identify the subject's natural source of interest

Before writing, identify what is intrinsically compelling about the material. It may be a documented scene, a contradiction, a mechanism in operation, a verified fact, a decision with uncertain consequences, a discovery, a process unfolding over time, or a question that challenges a familiar assumption. Choose the strongest entry point rather than trying to include every possible hook.

### Choose an opening mode that fits

- **Scene-led:** Use a documented scene involving a person, group, machine, place, event, or process. Begin at the point that best reveals the tension.
- **Contradiction-led:** Begin with a genuine gap between expectation and reality. State it clearly and support it with evidence.
- **Mechanism-led:** Show how a system, device, institution, or process works, what it must do, or where its limitations emerge.
- **Evidence-led:** Begin with a verified number, quotation, document, observation, or finding that reveals the scale or nature of the subject. Explain why it matters.
- **Question-led:** Introduce a specific mystery, dilemma, or unresolved problem that the script will genuinely investigate.
- **Person-led:** Use a real person when their documented actions or experience offer the strongest route into the subject. Do not introduce someone merely to personalize an abstract topic.
- **Process-led:** Begin with a process already unfolding, such as an outbreak spreading, a signal being transmitted, or a system reaching a critical threshold.

These modes can be combined. None is mandatory.

### Scene and factual integrity

If the opening uses a reconstructed historical scene, keep it within the evidence. Use present tense when it makes the scene immediate and the transition is clear. Sensory details such as sound, light, texture, temperature, or smell are optional and must not contradict the source. Do not add them merely to satisfy a quota. Do not invent dialogue, private thoughts, a witness, an operator, a victim, or a dramatic incident. A clearly labeled hypothetical illustration may be used when it genuinely clarifies a mechanism, but it must never be confused with a real event.

A location slug ending in «؛», a wide-to-close progression, an unnamed character, a delayed identity reveal, and cutting away before the outcome are optional scene-writing techniques, not required steps.

### Hook and narrative promise

Create a reason to continue listening through a genuine question, contradiction, uncertainty, consequence, or promise of explanation. One or two questions can work well, but no fixed number is required. The hook need not be a moral dilemma. It may be causal, scientific, historical, technical, political, or interpretive.

Clarify what the audience will understand by the end. For example: «این ویدیو، داستان [موضوع] است؛ داستان اینکه چگونه [فرایند یا تصمیم] به [پیامد] رسید و چرا نتیجه با انتظار اولیه تفاوت داشت.» Adapt or omit this formula when it sounds unnatural. Do not force every promise line to feature a character or bitter irony.

Connect the opening to the body naturally. A historical story may move backward in time; a mechanism-led story may explain how the system was designed; an investigation may establish the evidence and competing explanations. Do not insert an unrelated biography or a generic «تصور کنید مردی را…» passage simply because the subject is conceptual.

The body should deliver what the opening promises. The ending may return to the opening when the callback adds meaning, but it need not repeat the same scene or wording.

### Illustrative opening approaches

These are demonstrations of form, not factual accounts of particular incidents. Verify all topic-specific claims before using them in a real script.

**Autonomous weapons — mechanism and contradiction**

```
یک سامانه تسلیحاتی می‌تواند تصویر یک هدف را تشخیص دهد، مسیر حرکتش را پیش‌بینی کند و در چند ثانیه میان چند گزینه تصمیم بگیرد. اما تشخیص یک جسم با فهمیدن معنای حضور آن یکی نیست. مرز میان این دو، جایی است که بحث سلاح‌های خودمختار از مهندسی فراتر می‌رود و به مسئله مسئولیت می‌رسد. اگر تصمیم نهایی را ماشین بگیرد، چه کسی باید پاسخ‌گوی نتیجه باشد؟
```

**A plague — process and delayed recognition**

```
یک بیماری همه‌گیر لزوماً با لحظه‌ای آغاز نمی‌شود که شهر از خطر باخبر شود. ممکن است روزها بگذرد، در حالی که زندگی عادی ادامه دارد و نشانه‌ها هنوز به یکدیگر وصل نشده‌اند. مسئله فقط این نیست که بیماری چگونه منتقل می‌شود؛ مسئله این است که چه زمانی پراکندگی نشانه‌ها به الگویی تبدیل می‌شود که دیگر نمی‌توان نادیده‌اش گرفت.
```

**The dark web — system and misconception**

```
بخشی از اینترنت را نمی‌توان با همان روش‌هایی پیدا کرد که برای جست‌وجوی صفحات معمولی به کار می‌روند. اما پیدا نشدن یک صفحه در موتور جست‌وجو به این معنا نیست که آن صفحه مخفی، غیرقانونی یا ناشناس است. میان وبِ فهرست‌نشده، ابزارهای حفظ حریم خصوصی و بازارهای مجرمانه تفاوت‌هایی وجود دارد که اغلب در روایت‌های عمومی نادیده گرفته می‌شوند. برای فهمیدن این جهان، باید ابتدا مرزهای واقعی آن را شناخت.
```

These examples show that the same cinematic, suspenseful voice can work without an invented human protagonist. A person-led opening remains welcome when the documented person genuinely provides the strongest entry point.

## Narrator voice and tone

| Topic | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Point of view | Third-person omniscient; enter characters' minds only when the source supports it | First-person narrator, personal opinion |
| Channel presence | «ما» only in the narrative frame: «داستان ما»، «همان صحنه‌ای که داستان ما از آن آغاز شد» | «من»; any CTA |
| Mood | Serious, bitter, suspenseful | Jokes, casual register, promotional hype |
| Humor | Only situational irony: «قدرتمندترین امپراتوری جهان، برای برداشت چند جریب شلغم…» | Joking about characters or victims |
| Value adjectives | Backed by evidence: «منفور» followed by the fines for chickens and gates | Unsupported labels: «ذهن بیمار»، «هیولا» |
| Minor victims | Name, age and one human detail, such as the 12-year-old post boy | Faceless numbers |
| Judgment | Only in stage 8, attributed to groups: «برای ستایشگرانش… اما برای منتقدانش…» | Narrator verdicts mid-story |
| Concepts | Explain ideas in plain language through a relevant example, mechanism, event, or person | Textbook paragraphs, jargon, equations, or forced human anchors |

Contemporary Iranian political topics are more sensitive: attribute judgmental words such as «فرقه» or «خائن» to whoever said them, never to the narrator.

## Sentences, vocabulary and figures of speech

The prose is formal and literary, not bureaucratic. Average sentence length is 18–22 words; the rhythm comes from alternating long and short sentences.

**Rhythm**
- Long sentences (25–40 words) carry context and explanation; never go over 45 words.
- Short sentences (3–8 words) deliver impact, especially at the start or end of a paragraph: «آن‌ها فقط نگاه می‌کنند.»
- In climax scenes, chain short fragments: «صدای قدم‌ها در راهروها، صدای بحث‌های شبانه، صدای قلم روی کاغذ.»
- Use «نه‌تنها… بلکه…» and «نه… بلکه…» for contrast, at most three times per script.

**Verbs**: prefer plain verbs. Use bureaucratic forms at most two or three times per script, for variety only.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| محکوم گردید | محکوم شد |
| منصوب نمود | منصوب کرد |
| تبدیل ساخت | تبدیل کرد |
| به رشته تحریر درآمد | نوشته شد |
| مبادرت به … ورزید | … کرد |

**Superlatives and emphasis**
- Use at most 8 superlatives («…ترین») per script. When everything is "the most", nothing is.
- Use an adjective triad («سرسخت، اقتدارگرا و ستیزه‌جو») once or twice per script.
- Prefer numbers to adjectives: «۱۰ هزار پوند برای محصولی ۵۰۰ پوندی» beats «هزینه سرسام‌آور».

**Metaphor fields**: pick one or two per script and stay consistent.

| Field | Examples |
|---|---|
| Game / chess | شطرنج قدرت، مهره، کیش |
| Machine | ماشین سرکوب، ماشین تشکیلات |
| Slaughterhouse / hell | مسلخ، برزخ، دوزخ |
| Storm / fire | طوفان، گردباد، آتش، شعله‌ور |
| Shadow / ghost | در سایه‌ها، شبح، نامرئی |
| Fortress / siege | دژ، قلعه، سنگر |
| Dream / labyrinth | رؤیا، کابوس، هزارتو، دروازه ذهن |
| Church / faith | کشیش، کلیسای تازه، تکفیر، پاکسازی |

**Other devices**
- **Epithet instead of name** to avoid repetition: «مباشر لرد ارن»، «دانشجوی جوان»، «رهبر جنبش آزادی». Use the full name only at first mention.
- **Direct quotes**: three to six short quotes in «», only from the source, at key moments. A long quote, such as a speech, may be its own paragraph.
- **Historical irony**: one closing line as its own paragraph: «مردی که تمام عمر برای حفظ نظم ارباب‌رعیتی جنگید، ناخواسته نامش را به قدرتمندترین سلاح بی‌خشونت ضعیفان… بخشید.»
- **Precise numbers** for credibility: «هفده پلیس مسلح»، «۳۸۱ روز»، «۲ هزار و ۵۰۰ نفر».
- **Bridge to today**: one short paragraph linking the story to the present, such as AI-generated "dream" images after Surrealism or Gandhi and Montgomery after Boycott.

## Verb tense and narrative techniques

Narrate in the past tense. Use the present tense only for the cold open and the climax scene, and switch tense only at scene boundaries.

| Section | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open | Simple/continuous present | «باران سرد نوامبر بی‌وقفه می‌بارد.» |
| Background and escalation | Simple, continuous, past perfect | «او قاضی محلی هم بود و…» |
| Return to the climax | Present, after a date slug | «کاروان به لوف ماسک می‌رسد.» |
| Wrap-up and aftermath | Past | «وقتی حساب‌ها بسته شد…» |

Never change tense inside a paragraph. Typical past errors are «فرو رفته‌اند»، «نشانده‌اند» and «نبوده است» in the middle of past narration.

**Techniques**
- **Date/location slugs** on their own line, screenplay-style: «بیست و دوم سپتامبر ۱۸۸۰»، «دهم اکتبر ۱۹۲۱، وین»، «تابستان ۱۸۸۰»، «زمستان ۱۹۴۰، مارسی، ویلای اربل». Use 3–6 per script, only for key moments.
- **Countdown at the climax**, when the source gives times: «ساعت ۳:۲۰… ساعت ۳:۳۰ بعدازظهر…».
- **Sensory detail**: use sound, smell, light, texture, or temperature when they improve the scene and fit the evidence. No fixed number of senses is required.
- **Montage**: a list of short images can show time passing, repetition, acceleration, or pressure.
- **Withheld information**: delay a name or outcome only when the delay creates meaningful curiosity.
- **Foreshadowing**: anticipate a development only when the later narrative delivers on it.
- **Ring structure**: return to the opening when the callback reveals how the listener's understanding has changed. Do not repeat the opening merely to complete a template.
- **Reconstructed scenes**: use dialogue only when the source records it. When accounts differ, write «در یک روایت… در روایت دیگری…». For a doubtful but famous anecdote, write «به روایتی که بارها نقل شده…».

## Formatting and orthography

**File structure**
- Line 1: `# <video title>`. Nothing after it may be a heading, list, bold text or table.
- Paragraphs are separated by one blank line. Aim for 40–90 words each (the samples average about 50); 120 is the maximum.
- Date slugs and the cold-open location line sit on their own line, with a blank line before and after.

**Title patterns** (the subject, then «؛», then a question, as in both samples)

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Subject + question | کاپیتان بایکوت؛ چرا امپراتوری بریتانیا برای برداشت شلغم ارتش فرستاد؟ |
| Subject + "how" paradox | سوررئالیسم؛ چگونه رؤیاهای یک شاعر پاریسی دشمن فاشیسم شد؟ |
| Secret/superlative + life story | خطرناک‌ترین جاسوس دوجانبه؛ داستان زندگی کیم فیلبی |
| Contradictory role | چریکی که شوهرش را به جوخه ترور سپرد |

Write the title last so its promise matches the content.

**Punctuation**
- **«؛»** goes only between two complete, related clauses, or before an explanatory appositive: «این مرد، X است؛ مباشری که…». Never put it between subject and verb.
- **«،»** may also mark a narrator pause, but never between a short subject and its verb.
- **«»** marks quotes, nicknames, titles of works, operation names and mentioned words.
- **Never use parentheses**: the narrator cannot read them aloud. Write «، دانشگاه شریف امروز،» instead of «(دانشگاه شریف فعلی)».
- **No em dash (—)**: use a comma or «؛».
- **Ellipsis «…»** only inside quotes, to mark an omission.

**Spelling and consistency**

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| Digits | **Always** Persian digits (۱۲۳). Use digits for years, ages, sums and counts; write large numbers as «۴ هزار»، «۲ هزار و ۵۰۰»، «هشت میلیون». Spell out the day in date slugs («بیست و دوم سپتامبر ۱۸۸۰») |
| Gregorian months | ژانویه، فوریه، مارس، آوریل، مه، ژوئن، ژوئیه، اوت، سپتامبر، اکتبر، نوامبر، دسامبر (not جولای، می، آگوست) |
| Iranian topics | Solar Hijri dates («اردیبهشت ۱۳۵۴») |
| Half-space (ZWNJ) | می‌، نمی‌، …‌ها، …‌تر، …‌ترین and …‌ای take a ZWNJ; separate words take a normal space |
| Ezafe | No explicit mark: «خانواده بایکوت», not «خانواده‌ی» or «خانوادهٔ» |
| Latin script | **Never** inside the script; English names belong only in delivery notes |
| Names | One transliteration per name for the whole script; use the established Persian form where one exists (کا‌گ‌ب، سیا، فروید) |

## Factual accuracy and neutrality

Every number, date, name and quote comes from the source or from your verification searches, never from memory alone. The telling is dramatic; the facts are not.

1. **Verify key facts against two sources**: death dates, headline figures and central quotes. If you cannot verify one, say so to the user.
2. **Keep it internally consistent**: dates must agree (for example, the character's age in scene 1 = event year − birth year).
3. **No anachronisms**: organisation names and titles must belong to the period (the KGB did not exist before 1954).
4. **Title promise = content**: if the title says "the final 48 hours", narrate those 48 hours.
5. **Don't hide conflicting accounts**: give both, attributed.
6. **Limited reconstruction**: atmospheric detail (rain, light) is allowed if it doesn't contradict the source; invented dialogue or numbers are not.
7. **Doubtful figures**, such as a source mixing acres and hectares: drop them or soften them with «حدود».

**Neutrality in the ending**: always present two views, named by group or author, with similar weight (about 40–80 words each): «برای ملی‌گرایان و نویسندگانی چون مایکل داویت… اما مورخان امروزی…».

**Science, pseudoscience and conspiracy topics**: two-sided does not mean false balance. State settled scientific findings plainly; for example, homeopathic remedies perform no better than placebo in controlled trials. Attribute the claims to their believers. Move the closing dilemma to a genuinely open question, such as why people believe, who is harmed, or whether it should be regulated, banned or tolerated.

## Common mistakes (seen in earlier channel scripts)

| Mistake | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| CTA left in | A subscribe line after the promise, or "comment and like" at the end | Delete it; the script ends on the closing question |
| Lecture instead of story | A concept topic opened with a definition and then becomes a dry explanation | Find the subject's natural narrative entry point: a person, event, process, mechanism, contradiction, discovery, or consequence |
| Superlative inflation | 22 «…ترین» in one script | Max 8; replace the rest with numbers |
| Giant paragraphs | A 243-word paragraph | Split anything over 120 words |
| Tense slips | «فرو رفته‌اند» in past narration | Change tense only after a date slug |
| Inconsistent transliteration | مسالونی/مسولانی | One form for the whole script |
| Latin/foreign words | «مغز lavado» | Persian equivalent |
| Narrative gap | Jumping from 1951 to "final years" | At least one sentence per important period |
| Title/content mismatch | "48 hours" covering five days | Write the title last |
| Bureaucratic verbs | «تبدیل ساخت»، «منصوب نمود» | «کرد»، «شد» |
| Hook and ending disconnected | The closing question asks something the hook never raised | Reuse the second hook question almost verbatim |

## Word count check

Run this before delivering (on Windows use `python` instead of `python3`). It prints the word count excluding the H1, the longest paragraph, the superlative count, and paragraphs that open with «اما»:

```bash
python3 -I -c "import re,sys;t=open(sys.argv[1],encoding='utf-8').read();b=re.sub(r'^#.*\n','',t);p=[x for x in re.split(r'\n\s*\n',b) if x.strip()];print('words',len(b.split()),'max_para',max(len(x.split()) for x in p),'superlatives',len(re.findall(r'\S+ترین',b)),'ama_paras',sum(x.strip().startswith('اما') for x in p))" "<file.md>"
```

## Final checklist

- [ ] The Narration-Style folder was read in full before drafting
- [ ] The opening approach fits the subject's natural narrative potential
- [ ] No human protagonist, peak scene, location slug, sensory detail, or identity reveal was forced into the opening
- [ ] The hook establishes a genuine question, contradiction, uncertainty, or reason to continue
- [ ] The narrative promise matches what the body actually delivers
- [ ] A return to the opening is used only when it adds meaning; a circular structure is not mandatory
- [ ] **No CTA anywhere**: no subscribe, no related-video plug, no like/comment line
- [ ] 2,000–2,500 words (or the user's target); every paragraph under 120 words
- [ ] At most 8 superlatives
- [ ] Tense changes only at scene boundaries
- [ ] Every number, date and quote checked against the source
- [ ] Names and months consistent; no Latin-script words; no parentheses; Persian digits
- [ ] The ending reaches a clear, evidence-based conclusion; competing views, historical irony, and a final question are included only when they genuinely serve the subject
- [ ] The title matches the content; the filename has no forbidden characters
- [ ] Script read through once "aloud" for breath and rhythm

When delivering, tell the user briefly: the word count, the narrative approach used (for example, person-led, scene-led, mechanism-led, process-led, investigation-led, or chronological), the central question or tension, the source or research materials used, and any material facts that could not be verified. Do not assume every script has a central human character or a single climactic scene.
